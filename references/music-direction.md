# Background music direction

## Music serves the argument

Choose music after the narration cue map and section structure exist. The bed supports pace, contrast, and emotional framing; it must not dictate cuts or conceal factual meaning.

Use only original, commissioned, or licensed instrumental music with a recorded source and usage status. Never use a track because it resembles a named creator, film, or artist.

## Cue-map method

For every section, record in `music-cue-map.csv`:

- start/end cue and sequence IDs;
- narrative state: tension, investigation, explanation, consequence, release, or reset;
- relative energy (1–5), tempo feel, instrumentation role, and entry/exit action;
- track/stem source, license, edit/loop plan, and ducking target;
- whether music yields fully to narration, a quote, source audio, or silence.

Map energy to the editorial arc rather than holding one intensity throughout. A typical explanatory film uses contrast: restrained opening, controlled build into evidence, thinner bed under dense mechanisms or quotations, and a purposeful payoff or release. These are functions, not mandatory timings.

## Mix rules

- Narration is the priority whenever it carries a claim or number.
- Use sidechain ducking as the primary gain mover; do not stack an additional chapter-wide gain pass over an already scene-modulated track.
- Prefer stems or clean edit points over abrupt arbitrary cuts.
- Let source audio or silence take the foreground when it establishes evidence or a turn in the argument.
- Do not use a musical hit to manufacture importance for an unsupported claim.

## Approval condition

Music is approved only after direct listening to the delivered encoded mix confirms narration intelligibility, cue alignment, transitions, loudness, and the absence of unintended masking. A waveform or timeline is not an audio review.
