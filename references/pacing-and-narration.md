# Pacing and narration

## Start from meaning units

A meaning unit is the smallest narration span that changes the viewer's understanding. It can be a phrase, sentence, or short cluster; do not force every sentence into one shot.

For each unit, mark:

- **New information:** what becomes true or newly relevant.
- **Reading task:** what the viewer must see or compare.
- **Visual job:** proof, context, mechanism, comparison, consequence, or bridge.
- **Timing risk:** dense numbers, a quotation, a chart, a name, or a transition.

## Use a three-part beat

A readable explanatory beat usually has:

1. **Orient:** establish the object, axis, or question.
2. **Operate:** show the comparison or causal change.
3. **Land:** hold the consequence long enough to register before moving on.

The duration is determined by narration phrasing, information density, visual reading time, and contrast—not a universal seconds-per-shot rule.

## Density controls

- Introduce one new comparison axis at a time.
- When numbers matter, retain units, timeframe, population, and source context.
- Let a chart hold after its key change; animated arrival is not comprehension.
- If VO has moved on before the visual becomes legible, simplify the visual or revise the line.
- Use silence or reduced music to create room for a quote, inflection point, or document reading task.

## Audio hierarchy

Narration leads whenever it carries factual meaning. Music supports pace and section contrast without masking words. Clip sound is used when it supplies evidence or emotional context. Effects should clarify operations or transitions, not decorate every cut.

Review the encoded mix directly at normal playback speed. A waveform, transcript, or timeline cannot verify intelligibility.
